Diabetic Foot Ulcer Wound Care: First Steps and What Treatment Involves

7 min read

If you have diabetes and you have just found a sore on your foot, the most useful thing to know is that today matters. Diabetic foot ulcers are very treatable when they are caught early, and much harder to treat after a week or two of waiting to see what happens. This guide explains what an ulcer looks like, what to do the day you find one, and what specialist wound care actually involves.

Go to an emergency department now if

The wound comes with fever or chills, redness spreading up the foot or leg, foul-smelling drainage, or a foot that is hot, swollen and red. Those signs point to an infection that is already moving, and hours matter.

What is a diabetic foot ulcer?

A diabetic foot ulcer is an open sore that does not heal the way a normal cut or blister would. It most often forms on the ball of the foot, the tip of a toe, or underneath a callus at a pressure point. Ulcers form where pressure repeats and sensation is reduced, which is why they are so closely tied to diabetes.

Diabetes affects the feet in two ways at once. Nerve damage, called peripheral neuropathy, removes the pain that would normally warn you about a rubbing shoe or a stone in your sole. Changes in circulation slow the repair of any injury that does happen. Together, they let a small problem sit unnoticed in the tissue least able to heal it.

What does an early diabetic foot ulcer look like?

Early ulcers are easy to miss, especially on a foot that does not feel much. Watch for:

  • A callus with dark or reddish staining underneath it. That is an ulcer forming beneath skin that has not broken yet.
  • A blister, crack or scrape that has not started to heal within a few days
  • A spot that is warmer than the rest of the foot, which often comes before a visible sore
  • Drainage or a stain on your sock that you cannot explain
  • Redness, swelling, or a color change in one foot but not the other
  • Black or gray tissue at the edge of a wound

Do not wait for pain to tell you something is wrong. With neuropathy, the absence of pain is exactly why these sores go unnoticed.

Why do diabetic foot ulcers heal slowly?

Healthy healing needs four things: good blood supply, relief from pressure, control of infection, and body chemistry that cooperates, which includes blood sugar. A wound that lingers is missing at least one of them. Walking on an ulcer re-injures it with every step. Reduced circulation means fewer of the cells that fight infection and rebuild tissue reach the area. Blood sugar that stays high makes all of it harder.

That is also why a wound on a diabetic foot is rarely "just slow." Something specific is stopping it, and finding that blocker is what a wound specialist does.

What should you do the day you find one?

  1. Call a podiatrist the same day. Treat any ulcer as a same-day call, even if it does not hurt.
  2. Wash your hands before touching it, and keep the area clean and covered with a clean, dry dressing until you are seen.
  3. Stay off the foot as much as you reasonably can. Every step puts pressure back on the sore.
  4. Do not soak it. Skip baths, pools, hot tubs and the ocean, which is a real temptation in Jupiter and Palm Beach Gardens.
  5. Do not cut, pop or trim skin around it, and do not use over-the-counter corn removers, which are acid.
  6. Keep heating pads and hot water bottles away from your feet. With reduced feeling, you may not notice a burn.
  7. Take a photo. A daily picture makes it easier to tell whether the wound is changing, and it helps at your appointment.

Specialist wound care works through the blockers one at a time. At Abacoa Podiatry & Leg Vein Center, a treatment plan can include:

  • Offloading, redistributing pressure with total-contact methods, boots and footwear so the wound is not re-injured with every step

How long does a diabetic foot ulcer take to heal?

There is no honest single number. Healing time depends on the size and depth of the wound, circulation, infection, blood sugar, and how well pressure is kept off it. What is consistent is that ulcers treated early tend to close, and ulcers left alone tend to get deeper. As a working rule, any foot wound that is not clearly improving within two weeks needs a specialist, and any wound at all on a diabetic foot should be seen promptly.

How do you keep an ulcer from coming back?

A sore that keeps returning at the same spot is a sign that the pressure causing it has not been fixed. Once a wound has closed, prevention is the work: a daily look at the tops, soles and between the toes, shoes with a roomy toe box and no seams over pressure points, never walking barefoot, and orthotics or footwear that move load away from areas at risk. Anyone with a history of an ulcer should have a full podiatric exam every three to six months.

Can a diabetic foot ulcer heal on its own?

Do not plan on it. A diabetic foot ulcer is not a wound to watch for a week. Reduced sensation and slower healing mean an ulcer that is left alone can get deeper and reach bone. Contact a podiatrist the same day you find it.
