Preventing Complications: A Guide to Diabetic Foot Care

Reviewed by Dr. Orlando Cedeno, DPM · 7 min read

Preventing diabetic foot complications is mostly a handful of daily habits, done consistently, rather than any single dramatic intervention. If you already know the warning signs to watch for, this is the other half: the routine that keeps you from needing to spot them at all. Here is what that routine actually looks like, day to day.

The daily foot check

Two minutes, at the same time every day, ideally right after you take your shoes off for the evening so it attaches to a habit you already have.

  • Look at the top, sole, heel, and between every toe. Use a mirror propped on the floor, or ask a family member, if you cannot see the sole comfortably.
  • Compare one foot to the other. Redness, warmth or swelling in one foot but not the other is a meaningful signal.
  • Feel for a warm spot with the back of your hand. Inflammation raises skin temperature before it becomes visible damage.
  • Note anything different from yesterday, a new callus, a blister, a scrape you do not remember getting.

If you want the full list of what a problem actually looks like once you find one, our guide to common diabetic foot problems covers each condition and its warning signs in detail.

Footwear: the decision made every morning

More diabetic foot injuries start with a shoe than with anything else. A few rules that hold up:

  • Buy shoes later in the day, when feet have swelled to their largest, not first thing in the morning.
  • Roomy, rounded toe box. Nothing that tapers or presses on the smallest toe.
  • No seams across a pressure point. Run a hand around the inside of any new shoe before buying it.
  • Break new shoes in gradually, an hour at a time for the first several wears, checking your feet after each one.
  • Never walk barefoot, indoors or out, including at the pool or on the beach. This is a specific local risk in Jupiter and Abacoa, where warm weather and pool decks make barefoot walking constant unless you deliberately avoid it.
  • Check inside the shoe before every wear, for a stone, a folded insole, or anything that has worked its way through the sole.

Socks matter more than most people assume

Choose seamless, moisture-wicking socks without a tight elastic band at the top, which can restrict circulation without ever being felt. Diabetic-specific socks are padded at common pressure points and built without the irritating toe seam of an ordinary sock. Change socks daily, and mid-day as well if your feet sweat, since damp skin softens and breaks down faster than dry skin.

Nail and skin care, done right

Trim nails straight across, not curved, and stop at the corner rather than digging in, since that is how ingrown nails start. If you have reduced sensation, poor eyesight, thickened nails, or a history of cutting yourself while trimming, hand this task to a podiatrist. A small nail injury on a foot with reduced circulation can become a disproportionately large problem.

Moisturize daily, on the tops and soles, but keep lotion out from between the toes, where extra moisture invites fungal infection rather than preventing dryness. Cracked, dry skin is an entry point for bacteria, so this is prevention, not comfort.

Do not treat corns or calluses yourself with a blade, a razor, or an over-the-counter corn remover, which is an acid. Have them managed in the office instead.

What to do about warmth and pressure, not just what to look at

If you have neuropathy, consider a temperature-monitoring insole or mat, which flags an inflamed area days before it becomes a visible wound. Combined with the daily check, this catches the problem while it is still preventable. Call the same day if

You find any break in the skin, redness, swelling, warmth, drainage, or a color change, even if none of it hurts. Especially if it does not hurt. Neuropathy is exactly why pain cannot be the trigger for calling; the absence of pain is the reason these injuries get missed. See our wound care page for what happens at that visit.

Building the routine so it actually sticks

Most of this list fails not because any single step is hard, but because it depends on remembering all of it, every day, indefinitely. A few things make that realistic rather than aspirational. Attach the foot check to something you already do without thinking, taking off your shoes, brushing your teeth, so it does not depend on remembering a separate task. Keep a small kit by that spot: a hand mirror, moisturizer, and a fresh pair of socks, so nothing requires a trip to another room. And treat one missed day as nothing and three missed days as the trigger to reset the habit, rather than treating any single lapse as failure. The people who keep this up longest are not the most disciplined, they are the ones who made it require the least willpower.

How often to be seen

A full podiatric exam at least once a year if you have diabetes, and every three to six months if you already have neuropathy, poor circulation, foot deformity, or a previous ulcer. Prevention is a daily habit backed by a periodic professional check, not either one alone.
