Corns and Calluses

Corns and calluses are your skin's response to pressure. A podiatrist can trim them safely and look for the rubbing that keeps them coming back.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: clavus, heloma, soft corn, hard corn, callosity, hyperkeratosis

Corns and Calluses, explained

Corns and calluses are areas of thick, hardened skin that build up where something presses or rubs over and over. A callus is usually a broad, flat patch on the bottom of the foot or the heel and is often not painful. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons describes a corn as a small, circular thickening with a central core of hard material, and Mayo Clinic notes corns are smaller and deeper than calluses and can hurt when pressed. Soft corns can also form between the toes.

Anyone can get them, but Mayo Clinic notes they are more common with tight or poorly fitting shoes, going without socks, and foot shapes such as hammertoes and bunions, where a bent or bony toe rubs against the shoe. Corns and calluses are usually harmless in healthy feet. In people with diabetes or poor circulation, though, Mayo Clinic warns that even a minor foot injury can turn into an infected open sore, so they need more careful handling.

Common symptoms

  • A thick, rough or yellowish patch of skin
  • A small, hard, raised bump on the top or side of a toe
  • A soft corn between the toes
  • Pain or tenderness when the area is pressed or rubbed by a shoe
  • Dry, flaky or waxy looking skin over the spot
  • Redness, swelling or drainage around a corn, which can signal infection

Causes and risk factors

  • Shoes that are too tight, too loose or have a narrow, pointed toe box
  • High heels that push weight onto the ball of the foot
  • Wearing shoes or sandals without socks
  • Hammertoes, bunions and other toe deformities that rub against the shoe
  • Seams or stitching inside the shoe that rub one spot
  • An inherited tendency toward certain corns

How we treat it

Treating corns and calluses means removing the thickened skin safely and then reducing the pressure that created it. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons cautions that if the underlying cause is not treated, a corn may return. Mayo Clinic describes a doctor paring down thick skin with a scalpel, protective padding, shoe inserts and, when a bone alignment problem is causing the friction, surgery. The ACFAS and the NHS both advise against cutting corns at home, and people with diabetes or poor circulation should not treat them on their own.

Professional trimming

Your podiatrist can pare down a callus or remove the core of a corn with sterile instruments, which Mayo Clinic notes can be done during an office visit and the NHS lists as podiatry care.

Padding and protection

Felt pads, nonmedicated corn pads, toe separators or a little lamb's wool between the toes reduce rubbing on the spot, according to Mayo Clinic and the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons.

Shoe changes and inserts

Roomier shoes with a wider toe box and a lower heel, plus padded insoles or custom orthotics, spread pressure more evenly and help prevent the skin from thickening again.

Medicated softening, used selectively

Mayo Clinic notes that a doctor may apply a salicylic acid patch, but warns these products can irritate healthy skin and lead to infection, especially in people with diabetes. Your podiatrist decides whether it is safe for you.

Correcting the deformity

When a hammertoe or bunion keeps causing the same corn, Mayo Clinic notes surgery to correct the bone alignment may be an option, and our doctors perform foot surgery when it is indicated.

Diabetic foot checks and wound care

If you have diabetes or poor circulation, Mayo Clinic advises getting medical care before treating a corn or callus yourself, since even a minor foot injury can lead to an infected sore. Any open area under a corn or callus is treated as a wound.

Your first visit

At your first visit the podiatrist looks at where each corn or callus sits and what is rubbing it, checking your toes for hammertoes or bunions and your shoes for fit and wear patterns. They ask about diabetes, circulation problems and any home treatment you have tried, and look for cracks, redness or drainage, and check sensation and pulses. The doctor then explains your options, which may include trimming the thick skin, and how to keep it from returning.

Recovery

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons notes that a doctor may trim a corn to relieve pain, but the thick skin can rebuild if the pressure stays the same. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons notes that a corn may come back if its underlying cause is not treated, so long term results depend on footwear, padding and, in some cases, correcting a toe deformity. The NHS advises seeing a professional if a corn or callus has not improved after three weeks of self-care. If you have diabetes, Mayo Clinic advises seeing a provider before treating corns or calluses yourself.

What you can do at home

  • Wear roomy, cushioned shoes with a low heel and thick socks, and avoid tight or pointed shoes
  • Use nonmedicated felt pads, toe separators or a little lamb's wool between the toes to cushion the spot
  • Moisturize dry skin daily, but not between the toes
  • Do not cut, shave or dig at a corn or callus, and avoid medicated corn pads, which the ACFAS warns can lead to serious infection
  • If you have diabetes, poor circulation or a weakened immune system, skip all home treatment, including pumice stones and medicated pads, and have a podiatrist look at the area

When to get care right away

  • Any corn or callus if you have diabetes, poor circulation or a weakened immune system
  • Bleeding, pus, drainage or an open sore under or around a corn or callus
  • Redness, warmth, swelling or red streaks spreading from the area
  • Fever along with a painful, inflamed spot on the foot
  • Pain that limits your normal activity, or no improvement after three weeks of self-care

Corns and Calluses: common questions

What is the difference between a corn and a callus?

Both are thickened skin caused by pressure or friction. A callus is usually larger, flatter and less defined, and it often forms on the bottom of the foot or heel without much pain. A corn is smaller and rounder, with a hard central core that presses into the deeper skin, so it tends to hurt when pressed. Corns often form on the tops and sides of toes.

Can I cut off a corn myself?

It is best not to. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons warns against removing corns at home or using medicated corn pads because serious infection can occur, and the NHS also advises against cutting corns or calluses yourself. A podiatrist can trim the thick skin safely with sterile instruments and look for the pressure that caused it.

Are medicated corn pads safe if I have diabetes?

Medicated corn pads are not recommended for people with diabetes or poor circulation. They usually contain salicylic acid, which Mayo Clinic says can irritate healthy skin and lead to infection, especially in people with diabetes. Reduced feeling can also hide the damage until a sore has formed. Mayo Clinic and the NHS both advise these patients to see a professional instead of treating corns themselves.

Why do my corns keep coming back?

A corn is the skin's response to repeated pressure, so it returns if the pressure does not change. Tight shoes, high heels and toe shapes such as hammertoes or bunions are common reasons. The ACFAS notes that a corn may come back if its underlying cause is not treated. Changing shoes, adding padding or orthotics, or correcting the toe deformity addresses the source.

What causes a soft corn between the toes?

The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons explains that soft corns develop between the toes as the toes rub against each other, often inside a narrow or tight shoe, and that they can look like open sores. AAOS suggests a bit of lamb's wool (not cotton) between the toes to cushion them, along with shoes that have a roomy toe area. Because the skin there can break down, a painful or open soft corn should be checked by a podiatrist.

Can a callus on the foot turn into an ulcer?

In people with diabetes or poor blood flow, it can. Mayo Clinic notes that even a minor foot injury can lead to an infected open sore (ulcer) in these patients, and advises seeking medical care before self-treating a corn or callus. Reduced feeling can hide the problem, so a podiatrist can check the skin and look for the pressure behind the callus. Any drainage or open area should be seen promptly.
