Heel Pain Treatment in Jupiter, FL

"Heel pain" is a symptom, not a diagnosis, and at least seven different conditions cause it. The right treatment starts with knowing exactly which one you have.

How we treat heel pain

Once the cause is confirmed, treatment is matched to it, typically starting with the least invasive effective option:

  • MLS laser therapy for fasciitis, bursitis, and nerve irritation, painless and fast.
  • Shockwave therapy for chronic fasciitis and insertional tendon problems.
  • Orthotics and footwear strategy when mechanics are driving the overload.
  • Targeted stretching and strengthening matched to your diagnosis.
  • PRP or other regenerative options for resistant cases.
  • Immobilization for stress fractures, the one heel problem where rest truly is the treatment.

Is it really plantar fasciitis?

Plantar fasciitis gets blamed for nearly every sore heel, and much of the time the blame is deserved. But some patients arrive with that label after months of stretching and inserts that did nothing, and turn out to have something else. Treating the wrong condition doesn't just waste time; it can let a stress fracture progress or a nerve problem linger. If your pain does fit the classic first-step pattern, our plantar fasciitis treatment page covers home care, laser, shockwave, and PRP. Otherwise, these are the look-alikes we check for.

Achilles tendinitis and insertional Achilles tendinopathy

Location is the giveaway: plantar fasciitis hurts on the bottom of the heel, while Achilles problems hurt at the back, where the tendon attaches, or a few inches up the tendon itself. It's worst with the first steps of the morning, on stairs, or pushing off while running, and the back of the heel may be swollen or have a firm bump. Treatment focuses on eccentric calf strengthening, heel lifts, MLS laser, and shockwave.

Baxter's nerve entrapment

A small branch of the tibial nerve runs along the inner heel beneath the arch. When it's compressed, it causes pain on the inner bottom of the heel that mimics plantar fasciitis almost exactly. The difference is the quality of the pain: burning, tingling, or electric, sometimes radiating toward the arch or outer heel, and often worse at the end of the day rather than first thing in the morning. We diagnose it by exam and diagnostic ultrasound. Treatment includes offloading, targeted injections, and in persistent cases a minimally invasive nerve release.

Calcaneal stress fracture

The diagnosis we least want to miss. It develops from repetitive impact, typically in runners who increased mileage quickly or in people with low bone density. The pain is deep, aching, and diffuse rather than pinpoint, it hurts with every step instead of easing as you warm up, and squeezing the heel from both sides reproduces it. Early X-rays are often normal, so we rely on the squeeze test, ultrasound, and MRI when needed. Treatment is rest in a walking boot for four to eight weeks; shockwave, aggressive stretching, or running on a stress fracture can make it worse.

Heel fat pad atrophy

The fat pad under the heel absorbs shock with every step. With age, repeated cortisone injections, or years on hard floors, it thins. The result is a dull, bruised feeling in the center of the heel, worse on tile or concrete and better in cushioned shoes. Unlike plantar fasciitis it isn't worse in the morning, and stretching doesn't help. Ultrasound measures the pad's thickness. Treatment centers on cushioned heel cups, custom orthotics with a deep heel seat, and avoiding barefoot walking. Steroid injections make it worse.

Sever's disease in children and teens

Heel pain in an active child between roughly eight and fourteen is almost never plantar fasciitis. It's usually Sever's disease, an irritation of the growth plate at the back of the heel from the Achilles pulling during growth spurts. Kids limp after soccer, basketball, or gymnastics. It resolves once the growth plate closes, and in the meantime responds to heel lifts, calf stretching, activity changes, and sometimes a short period in a boot. If your child is limping, don't wait it out.

Haglund's deformity and retrocalcaneal bursitis

Sometimes called a "pump bump," Haglund's deformity is an enlargement of the bone at the back of the heel that rubs against the shoe. It irritates the Achilles and the bursa beneath it, causing pain, redness, and a visible bump. Stiff-backed shoes, dress shoes, and skates are the usual triggers. Treatment starts with footwear changes, heel lifts, and laser therapy for the inflamed bursa; persistent cases can be corrected with a minimally invasive procedure to reshape the bone.

Tarsal tunnel syndrome and referred pain

Tarsal tunnel syndrome is compression of the tibial nerve behind the inner ankle, similar to carpal tunnel in the wrist. It causes burning, numbness, or shooting pain into the heel and sole, often worse at night. Heel pain can also be referred from the lower back, or be an early sign of an inflammatory arthritis, especially when both heels hurt at once or the Achilles is swollen. When the exam doesn't fit a mechanical cause, we look further and coordinate with the right specialist.

How we tell them apart

We take a careful history: when the pain started, what it feels like, and what makes it better or worse. We examine the whole foot, ankle, and calf, not just the sore spot. Digital X-rays in the office show bone and alignment, and diagnostic ultrasound lets us see the plantar fascia, Achilles tendon, fat pad, and nerves directly. When the picture is still unclear, we order MRI or nerve studies. A correct diagnosis at the first visit is the fastest route to a pain-free heel, and it decides which treatment is actually going to work.

When to call right away

Some heel pain shouldn't wait for a trial of home care. Call us promptly if you have:

  • Heel pain after a fall or injury
  • Swelling, redness, or warmth over the heel
  • Numbness or tingling in the heel or foot
  • Pain that keeps you from bearing weight
  • Heel pain with a fever
  • Heel pain in a child that causes limping
FAQ

Heel pain questions, answered

What causes heel pain besides plantar fasciitis?

Common non-fasciitis causes include Achilles issues at the back of the heel, retrocalcaneal bursitis, Baxter's nerve entrapment, heel fat pad atrophy, stress fractures of the heel bone, and inflammatory arthritis. Each has different treatment, which is why pain location, timing, and exam findings matter so much.

Do heel spurs cause heel pain?

Usually not. Heel spurs are found on X-rays of many people with no pain at all, and most plantar fasciitis pain comes from the fascia, not the spur. Treating the soft tissue resolves the pain in the great majority of cases without ever removing the spur. A spur on the back of the heel near the Achilles is a different matter and can be relevant.

Can I have more than one of these at the same time?

Yes. Plantar fasciitis combined with Baxter's nerve entrapment or with fat pad atrophy is common, which is one reason a single treatment sometimes brings only partial relief.

Why didn't my cortisone shot help?

Cortisone treats inflammation. If the pain is from a nerve, a stress fracture, or a thinned fat pad, the injection won't help and in some cases makes it worse.

When should I see a doctor for heel pain?

See a specialist if heel pain lasts more than 1–2 weeks, keeps returning, disturbs sleep, follows an injury, or comes with swelling, numbness, or fever. Early evaluation shortens treatment and rules out the few serious causes.
