Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (Adult-Acquired Flatfoot)

An arch that slowly sinks, with pain along the inside of the ankle, can signal a failing posterior tibial tendon, and catching it early widens your options.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: PTTD, adult-acquired flatfoot, posterior tibial tendonitis, posterior tibial tendon insufficiency, progressive collapsing foot deformity

Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (Adult-Acquired Flatfoot), explained

The posterior tibial tendon connects a deep calf muscle to the bones on the inner side of your foot. It runs behind the bony bump on the inside of the ankle and then under the arch, where it acts as one of the main supports holding the arch up when you walk. In posterior tibial tendon dysfunction, the tendon becomes inflamed, stretched, or torn and gradually loses its ability to support the arch, so the foot flattens over time.

This is different from a flat foot you have had since childhood. It is acquired, and the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons describes it as typically progressive. According to NCBI StatPearls, it is seen most often in women and in people carrying extra weight, with the classic patient being a woman in her fifties. Diabetes, high blood pressure, earlier injury, and steroid use are other listed risk factors.

Common symptoms

  • Pain and swelling along the inside of the ankle and arch
  • Pain that is worse when walking, running, or climbing stairs
  • An arch that looks lower than it used to, often in one foot more than the other
  • The ankle rolling inward and the toes pointing outward
  • Weakness when pushing off or trouble rising up on the toes of that foot
  • In later stages, pain moving to the outside of the foot below the ankle

Causes and risk factors

  • Overuse of the tendon from walking, running, hiking, or long hours standing
  • Excess body weight
  • Being female and over about 50
  • Diabetes or high blood pressure
  • An earlier foot or ankle injury
  • Past steroid use or inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis
  • A flat foot structure that already strains the tendon

How we treat it

The goal is to take strain off the tendon and support the arch before the foot becomes stiff. NCBI StatPearls describes a conservative program of anti-inflammatory medication, a few weeks in a boot or cast, strengthening with physical therapy, and arch-supporting orthotics, usually over three to four months. StatPearls reports that about 89 percent of patients with stage I and II disease responded positively to orthotics and physical therapy. When the deformity advances or conservative care fails, the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons notes that surgery is recommended.

Boot or cast immobilization

A short period in a walking boot or short-leg cast rests the tendon and lets inflammation settle, which StatPearls places at about three to four weeks.

Orthotics and bracing

Arch-supporting orthotic devices or an ankle brace hold the foot in a better position so the tendon does not have to work as hard, according to ACFAS.

Physical therapy

Targeted strengthening, including eccentric exercises for the posterior tibial muscle, helps restore push-off strength, as described in StatPearls.

Anti-inflammatory medication

Medications such as ibuprofen can reduce pain and swelling in the early, inflamed stage of the condition.

Shoe modifications

Supportive shoes with room for inserts reduce stress on the inside of the foot during daily walking.

Reconstructive surgery

For advanced or nonresponsive cases, a foot and ankle surgeon may repair or replace the tendon, realign the heel bone, or fuse stiff, arthritic joints, depending on the stage.

Your first visit

Your podiatrist feels along the inside of the ankle for swelling and tenderness, then looks at both feet from behind. Seeing more toes than normal on the outer side, called the too many toes sign, suggests the arch is collapsing. You will be asked to rise up on the toes of one foot, since pain or inability to do this points to tendon weakness. The doctor checks whether the arch is still flexible and may order X-rays or other imaging.

Recovery

Recovery depends heavily on the stage. Cleveland Clinic says acute tendonitis can settle in a few weeks, while longer-standing tendon damage treated without surgery may take three or four months to fully recover. After surgery, Cleveland Clinic describes another two to four months of recovery and rehabilitation. NCBI StatPearls notes that surgical results are less predictable and some people have lingering symptoms even after reconstruction, which is one reason early evaluation is encouraged. Progress varies from person to person.

What you can do at home

  • Wear supportive, stable shoes rather than flat sandals or flip-flops, especially on long days standing
  • Cut back on long walks, hikes, and stair climbing until you are evaluated
  • Ice the inner ankle for short sessions with a cloth barrier
  • Rest your feet after strenuous activity
  • Notice whether one arch looks lower than the other and mention it at your visit

When to get care right away

  • Inner ankle or arch pain that lasts or keeps getting worse
  • An arch that is visibly flattening or a foot that is turning outward
  • You can no longer rise up on the toes of the affected foot
  • Sudden pain or a pop along the inner ankle, which could mean a tendon tear
  • You have diabetes and notice new foot swelling or a change in foot shape

Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (Adult-Acquired Flatfoot): common questions

Can posterior tibial tendon dysfunction be reversed?

In the early stages, when the arch is still flexible, many people improve with rest, bracing or orthotics, and physical therapy. NCBI StatPearls reports that about 89 percent of patients with stage I and II disease responded positively to orthotics and therapy. Once the foot becomes rigid or arthritic, the shape usually cannot be restored without surgery, which is why early care matters.

What is the too many toes sign?

The too many toes sign is a quick exam test for a collapsing arch. Standing behind you, the doctor counts how many toes are visible on the outer side of each foot. Normally only one or two show. According to NCBI StatPearls, seeing more than two toes flaring outward suggests the heel and forefoot have shifted outward because the arch is giving way.

Is posterior tibial tendon dysfunction the same as flat feet?

It is one specific cause of flat feet in adults. Many people are born with flexible flat feet that never hurt. Posterior tibial tendon dysfunction is different because the arch flattens later in life as the tendon weakens or tears, and it tends to progress. Pain on the inside of the ankle and an arch that is changing are the usual clues.

Do orthotics help posterior tibial tendon dysfunction?

Orthotics are one of the main nonsurgical treatments. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons lists orthotic devices and ankle bracing to support the arch, and NCBI StatPearls describes custom orthotics with added support under the inner forefoot. They reduce the load on the tendon, and they usually work best combined with strengthening exercises and supportive shoes.

How long is recovery after posterior tibial tendon surgery?

Cleveland Clinic describes about two to four months of recovery and rehabilitation after surgery for this condition, and more involved reconstructions can take longer. The exact plan depends on which procedures are done, such as tendon transfer, heel bone realignment, or joint fusion. Your surgeon will explain weight-bearing limits and physical therapy for your specific procedure.
