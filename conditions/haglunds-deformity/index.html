Haglund's Deformity (Pump Bump)

A firm, sore bump on the back of your heel that your shoes keep rubbing may be Haglund's deformity, and surgery for it is rarely needed.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: pump bump, Haglund's bump, posterior heel bump, retrocalcaneal exostosis

Haglund's Deformity (Pump Bump), explained

Haglund's deformity is a bony enlargement on the back of the heel bone, right where the Achilles tendon attaches. The bump itself is bone, but the pain usually comes from the soft tissue around it. When a stiff shoe back presses on the bump over and over, the small fluid sac between the tendon and bone (the bursa) can become inflamed, and the skin over it can turn red and swollen.

It earned the nickname pump bump because rigid-backed shoes such as pumps and dress shoes aggravate it, though ice skates and other rigid-backed shoes do the same. Cleveland Clinic notes that it is very common, more frequent in women, and often runs in families. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons links it to high arches, a tight Achilles tendon, and walking with extra weight on the outside of the heel.

Common symptoms

  • A visible, firm bump on the back of the heel
  • Pain where the Achilles tendon meets the heel bone
  • Swelling and redness over the bump
  • Pain that flares in shoes with a stiff or high heel counter
  • Skin irritation or color change over the bump
  • Symptoms in one heel or both

Causes and risk factors

  • Shoes with rigid backs, such as pumps, dress shoes, or ice skates
  • Inherited heel bone shape and family history
  • High arches
  • A tight Achilles tendon or tight calf muscles
  • Repetitive heel stress from running, especially uphill or on hard surfaces
  • Walking with weight on the outside of the heel

How we treat it

Treatment aims to calm the inflamed bursa and tendon and stop the shoe from rubbing, since the bone itself does not shrink on its own. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons lists anti-inflammatory medication, ice, stretching, heel lifts, padding, soft-backed shoes, physical therapy, orthotics, and sometimes casting. When pain continues despite this care, surgery to remove the bump and inflamed tissue is an option, though Cleveland Clinic notes it is rarely needed.

Shoe changes and padding

Switching to soft-backed or backless shoes and adding heel pads takes pressure off the bump, and Cleveland Clinic calls avoiding the shoes that trigger symptoms the most important way to manage them.

Heel lifts and orthotics

Heel lifts reduce pull on the Achilles, and orthotic devices or heel cups can improve how the heel sits in the shoe, according to ACFAS and Cleveland Clinic.

Stretching and physical therapy

Calf and Achilles stretching eases tension at the back of the heel, and physical therapy can add strengthening and other measures to reduce inflammation.

Anti-inflammatory medication and ice

Oral anti-inflammatory medication and ice can calm swelling of the bursa and surrounding tissue during flares.

Shockwave therapy (EPAT)

When the Achilles tendon is also irritated, AAOS OrthoInfo notes that studies of shockwave therapy for Achilles tendinitis show improved pain and function, though more evidence is needed. Abacoa Podiatry offers EPAT shockwave therapy.

Surgery

If conservative care does not relieve the pain, a foot and ankle surgeon can remove the bony enlargement and inflamed tissue, sometimes along with damaged tendon.

Your first visit

The podiatrist examines the back of your heel to locate the bump and pinpoint whether the tenderness sits over the bursa, the bone, or the Achilles tendon itself. Calf tightness, arch height, and the way your heel strikes the ground are checked, and the doctor looks at the heel counters of the shoes you wear most. X-rays may be ordered to see the shape of the heel bone and any spurring at the tendon attachment.

Recovery

Cleveland Clinic notes that most people feel better over time as they avoid the shoes or overuse that caused the pain, although the bump itself remains. If surgery is needed, Cleveland Clinic says recovery typically takes several weeks to a few months. When the procedure also involves the Achilles tendon, AAOS OrthoInfo reports that most patients walk in a removable boot or cast within two weeks, and that full rehabilitation can take up to 12 months to reach maximum improvement. Healing speed varies with age, health, and how much tissue is involved.

What you can do at home

  • Wear shoes with soft or open backs, such as clogs or supportive sandals, while the heel is sore
  • Place a soft pad or moleskin so it cushions around the bump rather than over it
  • Ice the back of the heel for short sessions with a cloth barrier
  • Gently stretch your calves and Achilles tendon each day
  • Avoid uphill running and hard surfaces until you are evaluated

When to get care right away

  • Heel pain that lasts more than a few weeks or keeps returning
  • Trouble standing, walking, or using your foot normally
  • Swelling at the back of the heel that does not go down
  • Sudden loss of foot function or severe pain, which Cleveland Clinic says warrants emergency care
  • A pop or sudden pain at the back of the heel, which could signal an Achilles tendon injury
  • Redness, warmth, or broken skin over the bump, especially if you have diabetes

Haglund's Deformity (Pump Bump): common questions

Will a Haglund's bump go away on its own?

The bony bump does not go away without surgery, because it is part of the heel bone. The pain, though, often settles once the inflamed tissue around it calms down. Changing to soft-backed shoes, padding, stretching, and anti-inflammatory measures relieve symptoms for many people, and Cleveland Clinic notes that surgery is rarely necessary.

What shoes are best for Haglund's deformity?

Shoes with soft, low, or open backs are usually most comfortable because they do not press on the bump. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons recommends soft-backed or backless styles and warns against rigid heel counters like those in pumps and dress shoes. Clogs, supportive sandals, and running shoes with a padded collar are common choices.

Is Haglund's deformity the same as a heel spur?

Not exactly. Haglund's deformity is an enlargement on the upper back of the heel bone, where it rubs against shoes and the Achilles tendon. A plantar heel spur forms on the bottom of the heel and is tied to plantar fasciitis. Small spurs can also form at the Achilles attachment, and AAOS OrthoInfo notes these often appear alongside insertional Achilles tendinitis.

Can stretching help a pump bump?

Stretching does not shrink the bone, but it can reduce the pain. A tight Achilles tendon pulls harder on the back of the heel and is one of the risk factors the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons lists. Daily calf and Achilles stretches, combined with shoe changes and padding, help ease pressure and irritation at the back of the heel.

How long does recovery from Haglund's surgery take?

Cleveland Clinic estimates several weeks to a few months of recovery after removing a Haglund's bump. When the Achilles tendon also needs repair, recovery is longer, and AAOS OrthoInfo describes up to 12 months of rehabilitation to reach maximum improvement. Your surgeon will explain boot time, weight-bearing, and physical therapy for your specific procedure.
