Wart or callus? Check first

Half the "warts" people treat for months are calluses, and half the "calluses" they file down are warts. Three signs settle it:

SignPlantar wartCallus
Black pinpoint dotsYes, clotted capillaries in the lesionNo
Skin linesGo around itRun straight through it
PainWorse squeezing the sidesWorse pressing straight down
SpreadsOften, in clusters (mosaic warts)No

A plantar wart is a skin infection with human papillomavirus, picked up from a wet floor or shared shower through a tiny break in the skin, then pushed inward by the weight of walking. That is why it can feel like a stone in your shoe and why over-the-counter treatments struggle to reach it.

How we treat plantar warts

The right option depends on how long the wart has been there, how many there are, and how much downtime you can accept. Most are handled in a short visit:

  • Laser wart removal. The laser targets the blood vessels feeding the wart, so it dies from the root rather than the surface. Often one to three sessions, walk out the same day. Our first choice for warts that have survived other treatments or keep recurring.
  • Cantharidin. A painless in-office application that lifts the wart in a blister over a few days. Well suited to children and to multiple small warts. Usually two to four visits.
  • Cryotherapy. Liquid nitrogen, colder and more precise than the pharmacy freeze kits. Stings for a moment, tender for a day or two.
  • Prescription-strength salicylic acid with regular in-office paring, for people who prefer a slower, gentler route.
  • Minor excision for the single large, long-standing wart where everything else has failed. Done under local anesthetic.

Whichever treatment we use, we treat the margin around the wart as well as the wart itself. The virus lives in the surrounding skin, and leaving it behind is the most common reason warts return.

Keeping them from coming back

  • Flip-flops in shared showers, locker rooms and around pool decks.
  • Dry feet thoroughly, fresh socks daily, and rotate shoes so they dry out.
  • Do not pick at or file a wart with tools you then use elsewhere; that is how one becomes six.
  • Cover a wart with a plaster while it is being treated so it does not seed the other foot.

When to see us rather than keep trying at home

  • It hurts to walk on.
  • It has been there more than two or three months, or it is growing or multiplying.
  • You have diabetes, poor circulation or neuropathy. Do not use acid or freeze kits on your feet at all in that case; the risk of a wound outweighs the wart.
  • You are not sure it is a wart. Some things that look like warts are not, and a specialist can tell in one look.
