Toenail Fungus (Onychomycosis)

Thick, yellowed or crumbly toenails are often caused by a fungal infection that rarely clears by itself, and a podiatrist can confirm it and start treatment.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: onychomycosis, tinea unguium, nail fungus, fungal toenail infection

Toenail Fungus (Onychomycosis), explained

Toenail fungus, or onychomycosis, is an infection underneath the surface of the nail caused by fungi, according to the American Podiatric Medical Association. Cleveland Clinic explains that dermatophytes, a type of mold that feeds on keratin, cause about 90% of toenail fungal infections. As the fungus settles into the nail and nail bed, the nail slowly thickens, changes color and can lift away from the skin beneath it.

Cleveland Clinic estimates that onychomycosis affects 1 in 10 people overall and about 1 in 2 people over 70. Fungi thrive in warm, damp places, so pool decks, gym showers and locker rooms are common sources. Florida heat, sweaty athletic shoes and barefoot time at community pools all give fungus a chance to spread. People with diabetes, poor circulation or athlete's foot face a higher risk.

Common symptoms

  • White, yellow or brown discoloration of the nail
  • A thickened nail that is hard to trim
  • Brittle, crumbly or ragged nail edges
  • Chalky or cloudy patches on the nail
  • Debris building up under the nail
  • The nail lifting away from the nail bed
  • An unpleasant odor

Causes and risk factors

  • Walking barefoot in damp public places such as pool decks, locker rooms and gym showers
  • Athlete's foot that spreads to the nail
  • Sweaty feet or shoes that trap moisture
  • A nail injury or repeated shoe pressure
  • Diabetes, poor circulation or a weakened immune system
  • Older age
  • Sharing towels or nail clippers

How we treat it

The American Academy of Dermatology notes that nail fungus almost never goes away on its own and can spread to other nails, so treatment is usually worthwhile. The first step is confirming the diagnosis, since conditions such as nail psoriasis can look similar. APMA states that FDA-approved oral antifungals may be the most effective treatment, and AAD describes prescription topical medicines and nail procedures as other options. Because toenails grow slowly, treatment takes months and the infection can return.

Lab confirmation

AAD explains that nail clippings or scrapings are examined under a microscope to confirm fungus and rule out look-alike conditions such as nail psoriasis.

Oral antifungal medication

Terbinafine or itraconazole is typically taken daily for 12 weeks for toenails, according to AAD, with blood tests to check liver function.

Prescription topical treatment

Efinaconazole and tavaborole solutions are applied daily for 48 weeks, and ciclopirox is a once-daily nail lacquer, per AAD.

Nail debridement

AAD lists trimming, filing and debridement of a thick infected nail among the procedures used alongside medication.

Nail removal

For chronic infections, APMA and AAD describe removing the nail surgically or chemically when other treatment is not enough.

Preventing reinfection

AAD notes that nail fungus can return, sometimes a year or more after clearing, so the doctor may recommend maintenance treatment and daily habits that keep feet dry.

Your first visit

At the first visit, the podiatrist looks closely at each toenail for thickening, color change, crumbling and lifting, and checks the skin between your toes for athlete's foot. The doctor asks how long the nails have looked this way, what you have already tried, and whether you have diabetes, circulation problems or liver concerns that affect medication choices. A nail sample may be taken to confirm fungus before prescription treatment begins.

Recovery

Treating toenail fungus takes patience. The American Academy of Dermatology says oral medication for toenails usually runs 12 weeks, and topical solutions are applied daily for 48 weeks. Even after the fungus clears, AAD notes it can take a year or longer for the nail to completely grow out, and a removed toenail takes 12 to 18 months to regrow. The nail may look abnormal for many months while healthy nail grows in. Results vary, and AAD cautions that the infection can return.

What you can do at home

  • Wear shower shoes at pools, gyms and locker rooms.
  • Keep feet clean and dry, and change socks when they get damp.
  • Trim nails straight across with clippers you do not share, and clean your tools after use.
  • Treat athlete's foot promptly so it does not spread to the nails.
  • If you have diabetes, have any new nail changes checked rather than treating them yourself.

When to get care right away

  • You have diabetes and suspect nail fungus
  • Redness, pain, swelling or pus around the nail
  • Bleeding around the nails or trouble walking
  • Nails that keep getting thicker, more discolored or misshapen despite self-care
  • Poor circulation or a weakened immune system along with nail changes

Toenail Fungus (Onychomycosis): common questions

Will toenail fungus go away on its own?

Rarely. The American Academy of Dermatology states that nail fungus almost never goes away on its own and can spread. APMA notes that daily cleansing and over-the-counter products may temporarily suppress a mild infection, though it can come back. AAD adds that nail fungus is treatable, especially when the infection is caught early and is mild, so an exam sooner is better than later.

Is toenail fungus contagious?

Yes. Cleveland Clinic describes many types of toenail fungi as quite contagious. According to the American Academy of Dermatology, you can pick it up by touching fungi on someone's skin or nail, sharing an unwashed towel or nail clippers, or walking barefoot on a pool deck, in a public shower or in a locker room. It can also spread from one of your nails to others.

Is oral medication for toenail fungus safe?

Oral antifungals such as terbinafine and itraconazole are FDA-approved, and APMA says they may be the most effective treatment. AAD notes that most side effects are mild, but because these drugs can affect the liver, your doctor will order blood tests to check liver function. Tell your podiatrist about your other medications and any liver history so the right option can be chosen.

Can toenail fungus come back after treatment?

Yes. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that nail fungus can return after clearing, sometimes a year or more later, and Cleveland Clinic says it often comes back. To lower the odds, AAD says a doctor may prescribe maintenance treatment for a year or longer, along with daily habits such as keeping feet dry, wearing shower shoes in public areas and treating athlete's foot early.

Could thick, discolored toenails be something other than fungus?

Yes. The American Academy of Dermatology explains that a nail sample is examined under a microscope partly to rule out conditions such as nail psoriasis, which can look very similar. Confirming the cause matters, because treatment for nail fungus often lasts months. Starting a long course of medication without knowing the nail is infected risks treating the wrong problem.
