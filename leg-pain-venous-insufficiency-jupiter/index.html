Leg Pain and Venous Insufficiency: What Your Legs Are Telling You

Reviewed by Dr. Orlando Cedeno, DPM · 7 min read

Leg pain has several genuinely different causes, and telling them apart by how the pain behaves, not just where it is, is the fastest way to know whether it's a wait-and-watch problem or something that needs attention now. This is a guide to what venous leg pain actually feels like, how it differs from the other common causes, and the specific red flags that mean don't wait.

What venous leg pain typically feels like

Chronic venous insufficiency happens when the one-way valves inside your leg veins weaken or stop closing properly, letting blood pool in the lower legs instead of moving efficiently back toward the heart. That pooling is what produces the pattern of symptoms people describe:

  • Aching or a dull heaviness, often described as legs that feel tired or "full" rather than sharply painful.
  • Throbbing, especially by the end of the day or after long periods of standing.
  • Swelling around the ankles and lower legs, usually worse by evening and better after a night's sleep.
  • Night cramps and restless legs, thought to relate to blood pooling through the day catching up once you're lying flat.
  • Itching or a mild burning sensation around visible veins or areas of skin discoloration.
  • Improvement with elevation, one of the more useful clues on its own: propping your legs up and feeling meaningfully better within twenty or thirty minutes points toward a venous cause.

Visible varicose veins often accompany this pattern but not always. Venous insufficiency can be present and symptomatic before any vein is visibly bulging under the skin.

How it's different from arterial disease

Venous problems are about blood struggling to leave the leg. Arterial problems are the opposite: blood struggling to get into the leg in the first place, usually from narrowed or blocked arteries. The symptom pattern flips accordingly.

Arterial leg pain, known as claudication, typically shows up during activity, walking a certain distance reliably brings on cramping or aching in the calf, and eases with rest. Elevating the leg, which helps venous pain, can make arterial pain worse, since raising the leg works against blood getting in rather than helping it drain. Arterial disease can also come with a leg that feels cold, looks paler, or has weak or absent pulses at the ankle, none of which are typical of a venous problem.

The two aren't mutually exclusive. Some people have elements of both, which is exactly why a pattern of symptoms described over the phone isn't a substitute for an actual exam and imaging.

DVT: the pattern that needs urgent care

A deep vein thrombosis, a blood clot in one of the deeper leg veins, can look similar to venous insufficiency at first glance, swelling, aching, discomfort, but the pattern and the urgency are different. DVT symptoms are usually:

  • One-sided, appearing suddenly in a single leg rather than gradually in both.
  • Accompanied by warmth and redness over the affected area, sometimes with visible swelling that's clearly asymmetric compared to the other leg.
  • Tender to touch along the calf, sometimes sharply so.

This combination needs same-day medical evaluation, not a wait-and-see approach, because a clot that breaks loose can travel to the lungs and become a pulmonary embolism, which is life-threatening. If leg pain or swelling is ever paired with chest pain, shortness of breath, or coughing up blood, that's a 911 emergency, not an urgent care visit, since those symptoms can mean the clot has already moved.

Go the same day for

Sudden swelling in one leg, especially with warmth, redness, or calf tenderness. Call 911 for leg symptoms plus chest pain, shortness of breath, or coughing up blood. Everything else in this article, the gradual aching, the evening swelling, the night cramps, is worth evaluating but is not an emergency pattern.

Other causes worth ruling out

Not every ache in the leg is vascular. Nerve compression in the lower back can refer pain down the leg in a pattern that mimics venous aching. Muscle strain and overuse produce soreness tied clearly to activity rather than time of day. Peripheral neuropathy, common with diabetes, produces burning, tingling, or numbness rather than the heaviness and swelling typical of venous disease. Part of what an evaluation does is separate these out rather than assuming leg pain automatically means veins.

How venous insufficiency is diagnosed

A venous duplex ultrasound is the standard, painless, and non-invasive way to check this. It uses sound waves to visualize the leg veins and watch blood flow in real time, showing whether the valves are closing properly or letting blood reflux backward when they shouldn't. The same scan checks for a blood clot, which is useful since clot and chronic venous insufficiency can overlap in how they present. Combined with a physical exam and a history of when and how your symptoms show up, this usually gives a clear answer without any guesswork.

When to see someone

See a provider if aching, heaviness, or swelling has become a regular pattern rather than an occasional after-a-long-day thing, if it's gradually getting worse, if you're noticing new varicose veins or skin changes around the ankles, or if compression stockings and elevating your legs at the end of the day aren't keeping it under control. Left unaddressed, chronic venous insufficiency can progress to skin changes and, eventually, venous ulcers that are slow to heal, so earlier evaluation tends to mean simpler treatment. See our venous insufficiency page for more on how it's evaluated and treated.

Once venous insufficiency is confirmed, treatment ranges from compression and lifestyle changes through minimally invasive procedures. Our page on how EVLT works covers the laser-based option for closing off a faulty vein, and sclerotherapy before and after covers a common option for smaller veins. For more severe or advanced cases, vein ligation addresses larger, symptomatic veins directly, and our piece on exercise and movement for leg vein health covers what you can do day to day alongside any procedure.
