Neuropathy in the Feet: Symptoms, Causes, and When to See a Podiatrist

7 min read

Neuropathy in the feet usually starts quietly. A little tingling in the toes at night. A patch of skin on the sole that feels numb. The sense that your sock has bunched up under your foot when it has not. Because the early signs are easy to shrug off, many people live with them for months before asking what is going on. This guide covers what the symptoms of peripheral neuropathy feel like, what commonly causes it, and when it is worth having your feet examined.

What is neuropathy in the feet?

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the nerves farthest from your spine. The nerves that run to your feet are the longest in the body, so the feet are usually where it shows up first. A damaged nerve can misfire, which produces burning, tingling or jolts of pain. It can also go quiet, which produces numbness and makes balance harder.

Symptoms often begin in the toes and creep upward over months or years in what is called a stocking pattern, covering roughly the area a sock would. Patients often describe it as wearing a sock they cannot take off.

What are the first signs of neuropathy in the feet?

The early symptoms vary from person to person, but these are the most common:

  • Tingling or pins and needles in the toes or across the sole
  • Burning, often worse at night or after a long day
  • Numb patches, or toes that feel "dead"
  • Electric, shooting or stabbing jolts that come and go
  • A feeling of walking on socks, pebbles or cotton wool
  • Feet that do not sense hot and cold properly
  • Unsteadiness, especially walking in the dark

These usually come on gradually and tend to affect both feet. Some people mainly notice pain. Others lose feeling with very little pain at all, and that second group is at more risk, because nothing prompts them to look at their feet. A blister from new sandals or a stone in a shoe can sit there for an entire day without being felt.

What does neuropathy pain feel like?

People describe it in very different words, which is part of why it gets missed. Some call it a burn, like a sunburn on the soles. Some say their feet buzz or fizz, as if they had fallen asleep and never fully woke up. Others describe sudden zaps that make them flinch, or a tightness, as if the foot is wrapped too firmly.

Symptoms are often worst at night. Some people find the burning keeps them awake even though their feet felt manageable during the day. If you recognize that pattern, it is worth mentioning to a doctor rather than waiting for it to become constant.

What causes neuropathy in the feet?

Diabetes is the most common cause. Sustained high blood sugar damages nerves and the small blood vessels that feed them, and the feet show it first. It is far from the only cause, though. Chemotherapy, vitamin deficiencies such as low B12, thyroid conditions and certain medications can all damage nerves, and in some people no cause is ever found.

The cause matters because it changes the outlook. Neuropathy from a correctable cause can improve substantially once that cause is treated. With diabetic neuropathy, blood sugar control slows progression more than any other single step. That is why part of a good evaluation is screening for causes that can be treated, not only managing the symptoms.

Could it be something other than neuropathy?

Not every burning or tingling foot is peripheral neuropathy. A single nerve can be pinched in one spot, such as the nerve on the inner side of the heel, and that produces symptoms in one area rather than across both feet. Poor circulation is another possibility: cold feet, skin that has turned shiny or hairless, and cramping in the calf when walking that eases with rest are signs of reduced blood flow, which needs its own workup. An exam sorts these out, because the treatment for each is different.

When should you see a podiatrist for neuropathy symptoms?

Make an appointment if you notice new numbness, burning or tingling in your feet, especially if it is spreading or affecting your sleep or balance. If you have diabetes, do not wait: numbness plus diabetes needs prompt specialist care.

Some situations call for faster action. Call promptly if you have numb feet and also find any open sore, crack or blister, or redness, warmth or swelling, or a color change in the skin or toes. Seek urgent care for spreading redness or red streaks, fever with foot pain, or a foot that turns cold or blue.

If you have diabetes, a full foot exam at least once a year is the baseline, and every three to six months once neuropathy is present.

How is neuropathy in the feet treated?

Treatment depends on the cause and on what is bothering you most. The usual starting point is nerve-pain medication such as gabapentin, pregabalin or duloxetine. Daily habits that protect numb feet

Whatever the cause, feet that cannot feel properly need a little extra care every day:

  • Look at the tops, soles, heels and between the toes once a day. A mirror on the floor or a family member helps with the soles.
  • Run a hand inside each shoe before you put it on, feeling for stones or a folded insole.
  • Do not walk barefoot, indoors or out, including on pool decks and the beach.
  • Skip heating pads and hot water bottles on your feet. With neuropathy you may not feel a burn until it has happened.
  • Leave corns and calluses to the office rather than trimming them yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Can neuropathy in the feet be reversed?

It depends on the cause and how early it is caught. Neuropathy from a treatable cause, such as a B12 deficiency, a thyroid problem or a medication, can improve substantially when that cause is corrected. Diabetic neuropathy can be slowed, and symptoms often reduced, with good blood sugar control plus supportive treatment. Nerves that still partly work respond far better than nerves that have been damaged for a long time.

Should I see a podiatrist or my primary care doctor for neuropathy?

Often both. Neuropathy in the feet is a symptom problem and a safety problem. A podiatrist treats the discomfort and protects you from what numbness invites, such as unnoticed wounds, deformity and ulcers, with skin checks, footwear guidance and preventive care.
