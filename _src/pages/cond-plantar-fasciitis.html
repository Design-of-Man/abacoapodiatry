What is plantar fasciitis?

Plantar fasciitis is irritation and degeneration of the plantar fascia, the thick band of tissue that runs from your heel bone to your toes and supports your arch. Every step you take, that band absorbs load. When the load exceeds what the tissue can tolerate, from standing all day, ramping up running, tight calves, unsupportive footwear, or foot mechanics, tiny tears accumulate faster than they can heal.

The tell-tale sign: sharp heel pain with your first steps in the morning (or after sitting), easing as you warm up, then returning after rest. That happens because the fascia tightens while you're off your feet, and those first steps re-stretch the injured tissue.

Why it becomes chronic

Here's the part most patients are never told: in long-standing cases, the problem stops being inflammation ("-itis") and becomes degeneration ("-osis"). The fascia develops disorganized, poorly vascularized tissue that has essentially stopped healing. That's why rest, ice, and anti-inflammatories, all inflammation tools, quit working on chronic cases. The tissue doesn't need calming down. It needs to be provoked into healing again. That's exactly what shockwave, laser, and PRP do.

Why it's so common in Jupiter and Palm Beach Gardens

Our patients tend to share a few risk factors. Flip-flops, slides, and flat sandals worn year-round give the arch almost no support. Tile and concrete floors are hard on the heel. Sudden jumps in activity, such as starting a walking program, picking up pickleball, or training for a race, overload the fascia faster than it can adapt. Tight calves, weight gain, flat feet, very high arches, and jobs that keep you standing all day (nursing, teaching, retail, restaurants) all add to the strain.

What to try at home first

For mild heel pain that started recently, these steps settle a large share of cases within a few weeks:

  • Stretch the calf and plantar fascia several times a day. Before you get out of bed, pull your toes back toward your shin and hold for 30 seconds. Repeat three times.
  • Stop going barefoot on hard floors. Wear a supportive shoe or a recovery sandal with an arch inside the house.
  • Retire worn-out shoes. Running and walking shoes lose their support long before they look worn, and a quality over-the-counter arch support helps in every pair.
  • Ice after activity. Rolling the arch over a frozen water bottle for about 15 minutes combines icing with massage.
  • Reduce activity, don't stop it. Swap running for cycling or swimming for two to three weeks.
  • Try a night splint if mornings are the worst part. It keeps the fascia lengthened while you sleep.

If you're not clearly improving after two to three weeks of consistent home care, it's time to be seen. Heel pain that lingers past three months tends to become chronic, and chronic plantar fasciitis is harder to treat.

Making sure it's plantar fasciitis

Several heel problems look like plantar fasciitis but don't respond to the same treatment, including Baxter's nerve entrapment, calcaneal stress fractures, and heel fat pad atrophy. So every plan starts with an exam, a look at your activity and footwear, and in-office digital X-ray and diagnostic ultrasound when needed. Ultrasound shows the fascia itself: we can measure its thickness and look for tears. If the picture doesn't fit, our heel pain page walks through the other causes we check for.

Laser, shockwave and PRP for heel pain that won't quit

After roughly three to six months the fascia changes: the collagen becomes disorganized, blood supply drops, and the tissue loses its ability to repair itself. Each of these in-office treatments gives it a signal to heal again, and they're often combined.

MLS laser therapy

MLS (Multiwave Locked System) delivers two synchronized wavelengths of FDA-cleared laser energy, one aimed at inflammation and swelling, the other at pain. Most patients feel nothing more than mild warmth, there's no downtime, and you walk out in your normal shoes. It suits patients who want to avoid injections, those who can't take anti-inflammatory medication, and heel pain that's still in the inflammatory phase or comes with Achilles tendinitis. A typical course is 6–12 sessions, and many patients notice a change by the third or fourth visit. More on MLS laser therapy.

EPAT shockwave therapy

A handpiece placed against the heel sends rapid acoustic pressure waves into the damaged fascia. That controlled stimulus draws new blood flow and repair activity back to tissue whose healing had stalled. It's uncomfortable but tolerable, the intensity is adjustable, and no anesthesia is needed. A typical course is three to five weekly sessions; improvement usually begins two to six weeks after the last one and keeps building for months as the tissue remodels. It's where we usually start when pain has lasted more than six months, when one or more cortisone injections have already been tried, or for athletes who need to keep training. More on shockwave therapy.

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP)

We draw a small amount of your blood, spin it to concentrate the platelets, and inject that concentrate into the damaged part of the fascia under ultrasound guidance. Platelets carry growth factors that support tissue repair. Because PRP is made from your own blood there's no risk of allergic reaction, and unlike cortisone it doesn't suppress the healing response. It's typically a single injection: expect a few days of soreness and a short period in a walking boot, with results building over 8–12 weeks. More on PRP therapy.

Which one fits your case?

  • Pain under three months, still inflammatory, no needles wanted: MLS laser, plus orthotics and a stretching program.
  • Pain over six months, a cortisone shot already tried, active or athletic: shockwave, often paired with MLS laser between sessions.
  • Severe pain, a partial tear on ultrasound, or shockwave that didn't help: PRP, followed by a structured return-to-activity plan.
  • Any of the above without fixing footwear, mechanics, and calf tightness: expect the pain to come back. Custom orthotics and a home program are part of every plan.

Custom orthotics made from a digital scan of your feet redistribute pressure more precisely than generic inserts, which helps prevent flare-ups. Targeted stretching, gait analysis, and hands-on therapy remain the foundation, and they tend to work better alongside laser or shockwave than on their own.

What about surgery?

Very few patients ever need it. For the rare case that fails everything above, minimally invasive options such as ultrasound-guided percutaneous fasciotomy release tension on the fascia through a tiny incision, with a faster recovery than traditional open surgery.

FAQ

Plantar fasciitis questions, answered

Will it go away on its own?

Sometimes, eventually. But "eventually" routinely means 6–18 months of limping, and the longer it persists, the more the tissue degenerates and the harder it is to treat. Early treatment is dramatically faster and cheaper than late treatment.

Is a heel spur causing my pain?

Probably not. Heel spurs show up on X-rays of many pain-free feet; they're usually a result of chronic fascia tension, not the cause of pain. Treating the fascia treats the pain. Spur removal is rarely necessary. More in our heel pain guide.

Should I stop exercising?

Total rest usually backfires, tissue heals with the right amount of load, not zero load. We'll help you modify activity (bike and swim usually stay; barefoot beach runs pause) while treatment rebuilds the fascia's capacity. Low-impact activity is generally fine throughout laser and shockwave treatment; after PRP, expect about two weeks of reduced activity.

Does shockwave therapy hurt?

It's uncomfortable for the few minutes of treatment, like a firm, rapid tapping on the sore spot. The intensity is adjustable and most patients tolerate it well without anesthesia.

Can I do laser and shockwave together?

Yes. Combining MLS laser with shockwave is common in our office, with laser sessions between the shockwave visits.

How many cortisone injections are too many?

We rarely recommend more than one or two in the heel. Repeated steroid injections weaken the fascia, thin the heel fat pad, and raise the risk of a plantar fascia rupture. More in steroid injection for plantar fasciitis.

Do I need an X-ray?

Not always, but it's often useful to rule out a stress fracture or other bone problem. Digital X-ray and diagnostic ultrasound are in the office, so the answer is usually available the same day.

What results do laser and shockwave actually get?

Published studies of shockwave for chronic plantar fasciitis consistently show significant pain reduction versus placebo, and laser therapy studies report high patient-improvement rates. In our clinic, most patients feel meaningful change within the first weeks of a program. Individual results vary, which is why we track your progress and adapt.
