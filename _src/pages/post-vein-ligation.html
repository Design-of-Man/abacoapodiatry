The Role of Vein Ligation in Treating Severe Varicose Veins

Reviewed by Dr. Orlando Cedeno, DPM · 5 min read

Severe varicose veins are more than cosmetic — they can bring real pain, swelling, skin changes, and in advanced cases, ulcers. Vein ligation is one of the more established treatments for addressing the underlying cause rather than just the visible symptom.

What's driving severe varicose veins

Varicose veins develop when faulty valves let blood flow backward and pool, causing the vein to enlarge and bulge under the skin. In more severe cases, that can mean leg pain and cramping, swelling in the legs and ankles, throbbing or burning, skin discoloration or thickening, and painful venous ulcers that are slow to heal. When compression stockings and lifestyle changes aren't enough to manage that, ligation becomes a reasonable next step.

How the procedure works

Vein ligation ties off the affected vein to stop blood from flowing through it, relieving the pressure that causes the bulging and discomfort. The process typically involves local or general anesthesia depending on the extent of the veins involved, small incisions near the top and bottom of the affected vein, tying it off at both ends, and in some cases removing the vein entirely through the same incisions (vein stripping). Afterward, the body naturally redirects blood flow through healthier veins.

Who it's for

Good candidates typically have large, bulging varicose veins causing real discomfort, complications like ulcers or blood clots, no meaningful improvement from conservative treatment, and overall health that supports a surgical procedure. A vein specialist evaluates your specific case to confirm it's the right fit.

What patients get from it

  • Long-lasting symptom relief — reduced pain, swelling, and discomfort that lets patients return to normal activity.
  • Minimally invasive — small incisions generally mean less scarring and a faster recovery than more extensive vascular surgery.
  • Complication prevention — addressing the root cause helps prevent ulcers, clots, and further skin changes.
  • Improved appearance — a secondary benefit alongside the primary medical relief.

Recovery

Mild bruising, swelling, and discomfort around the incisions are typical in the first few weeks. Walking regularly supports circulation and helps reduce clot risk, while heavy lifting and strenuous activity are avoided until your provider clears you. Most patients return to normal activity within a few days to a few weeks, depending on the extent of the procedure.
