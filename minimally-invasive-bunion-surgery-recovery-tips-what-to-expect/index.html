Minimally Invasive Bunion Surgery: Recovery Tips and What to Expect

Reviewed by Dr. Orlando Cedeno, DPM · 6 min read

A bunion is a progressive deformity, not just a cosmetic issue — and if you're considering surgery, the recovery timeline is usually the first real question. Minimally invasive techniques have changed what that timeline looks like for the right candidate.

Traditional vs. minimally invasive bunion surgery

Traditional bunionectomy uses a larger incision to access the bone and joint, with procedures like osteotomy (cutting and realigning bone), exostectomy (shaving down the bony bump), or arthrodesis (fusing the joint in severe cases). Recovery generally runs 6–8 weeks or more with limited weight-bearing.

Minimally invasive bunion surgery uses tiny incisions, typically 2–5mm, guided by fluoroscopic imaging to correct alignment without opening the joint. That means less tissue trauma, minimal scarring, a lower infection risk, and a meaningfully faster return to walking.

The recovery timeline, week by week

Weeks 1–2: a post-op shoe or boot, elevation and ice to manage swelling, pain generally well controlled with medication, and gentle toe movement to preserve flexibility.

Weeks 3–6: gradually increasing weight-bearing depending on the procedure, stitch removal if needed, decreasing swelling, and physical therapy to rebuild strength and mobility. Patients who had minimally invasive surgery often return to regular shoes within 2–4 weeks — notably faster than the traditional timeline.

Weeks 6–12: a return to regular, supportive shoes, resolving swelling, resumed low-impact activity like walking or cycling, and for most patients, a full return to work and daily routines.

Tips for a smoother recovery

  • Follow your surgeon's specific instructions on weight-bearing limits, wound care, and when to begin movement or therapy — every foot and every procedure is different.
  • Wear supportive shoes with a wide toe box, avoiding heels or narrow shoes for at least 2–3 months.
  • Elevate and ice regularly, especially in the first 10 days, to reduce swelling and speed tissue healing.
  • Stay gently mobile to prevent stiffness and support circulation, using crutches or a knee scooter if recommended.
  • Ask about post-surgical laser therapy, which can help reduce inflammation and support healing as an adjunct to standard recovery care.

Who's a good candidate for the minimally invasive approach

Not every bunion qualifies. Good candidates typically have mild to moderate deformity, no significant arthritis in the big toe joint, healthy bone and skin, and a willingness to follow the post-op care plan closely. A proper evaluation is what determines whether the minimally invasive route or a more traditional approach will get you the better outcome.
