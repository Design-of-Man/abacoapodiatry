Flat Feet & Achilles Pain: Best Shoes & Relief Tips

Reviewed by Dr. Orlando Cedeno, DPM · 6 min read

Achilles pain that keeps coming back often isn't an Achilles problem at all — it's a foot-shape problem showing up in the tendon. If you have flat feet, here's the mechanical chain that connects the two, and what actually breaks it.

The mechanics: how a flat arch strains the Achilles

Flat feet — also called fallen arches — happen when the arch collapses partially or entirely, so the whole sole touches the ground. Some people are born with them; others develop them from aging, weight gain, pregnancy, tendon weakening, or years in unsupportive footwear.

In a foot with a normal arch, the arch supports alignment and spreads pressure evenly. In a flat foot, the heel tilts outward with every step — a pattern called overpronation — and that tilt places the Achilles tendon under abnormal, uneven strain instead of the straight pull it's built to handle. Repeated over thousands of steps a day, that strain is exactly how flat feet turn into Achilles tendonitis.

Early signs worth acting on

Catching this early is much easier to manage than waiting for it to become a real problem:

  • Morning stiffness in the heel or Achilles
  • Mild aching after walking or standing that used to feel fine
  • A heel that visibly tilts inward when you look at your feet from behind
  • Shoes wearing unevenly on the inside edge

What actually helps

Stretching the Achilles and calves. Consistent daily stretching — both straight-knee and bent-knee calf stretches — keeps the tendon and surrounding muscles flexible, which reduces the strain a flat arch places on them.

Strengthening the arch. Targeted foot and ankle strengthening builds better support from the muscles themselves, which can reduce how much the arch collapses under load.

Supportive footwear — the make-or-break factor. Look for firm arch support, a heel counter stable enough to resist tilting outward, and a slight heel-to-toe drop, which takes some tension off the Achilles directly. A shoe that was supportive when new stops being supportive once worn out — replace running or walking shoes every 300–500 miles regardless of how good they still look.

Custom orthotics. Orthotics correct the overpronation itself, addressing the mechanical cause rather than just managing the resulting tendon pain — which is why they're often more effective long-term than shoes or stretching alone.

Laser therapy. For Achilles pain that's already inflamed, MLS laser therapy reduces inflammation and pain directly, and works well alongside the footwear and orthotic changes that address the underlying cause.

Long-term prevention

Once pain is under control, the same tools — supportive footwear, orthotics if the flat-foot pattern is significant, and ongoing calf flexibility — are what keep it from returning. Flat feet themselves usually don't change without surgery, so the strategy is managing the mechanics around them, consistently, rather than solving the arch itself.

When to see a specialist

If early signs don't improve with better shoes and stretching within a few weeks, or if pain is limiting activity, it's worth a proper evaluation — flat feet vary enough in severity that what works for a mild case may not be enough for a more significant one.
