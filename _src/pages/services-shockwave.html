What is shockwave therapy?

Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (also called EPAT — Extracorporeal Pulse Activation Technology) is a non-invasive treatment that sends acoustic pressure waves into chronically injured tissue to stimulate circulation, break down scar tissue, and reactivate the body's healing response.

Here's why that matters: acute injuries heal because the body mounts an aggressive repair response. But in chronic conditions — plantar fasciitis that's dragged on for a year, an Achilles tendon that aches every morning — that repair response has shut down. The tissue is degenerated, poorly vascularized, and full of disorganized scar tissue. Rest won't fix it. Ice won't fix it. The body has simply stopped trying.

Shockwave gives it a reason to try again. The pressure waves create controlled microtrauma that triggers new blood vessel formation, releases growth factors, and stimulates cells called fibroblasts to rebuild healthy collagen. It is one of the most researched non-surgical treatments in orthopedics, with strong evidence in chronic plantar fasciitis and tendinopathy.

What we treat with shockwave

  • Chronic plantar fasciitis — including cases that failed injections
  • Achilles tendinopathy (insertional and mid-portion)
  • Stubborn heel pain and heel spur syndrome
  • Other chronic tendinopathies of the foot and ankle
  • Overuse sports injuries that won't resolve

FAQ

Shockwave questions, answered

Does shockwave therapy hurt?

You'll feel a firm, rhythmic tapping at the treatment site. Most patients rate it as mildly uncomfortable at worst, and we adjust intensity to your tolerance. Any post-treatment soreness is usually gone within a day or two — a sign the healing response has been activated.

How is shockwave different from laser therapy?

Shockwave uses mechanical acoustic energy to provoke a healing response in chronically degenerated tissue; laser uses light energy to boost cellular repair and calm pain and inflammation. Shockwave is typically the heavier hitter for old scarred injuries; laser is gentler and broader. Many patients get both — see our combined programs.

When will I feel better?

Some patients feel improvement after the first session, but shockwave's real gains appear over the weeks after the course finishes, as new tissue rebuilds. Studies commonly measure outcomes at 12 weeks — patience pays off with this therapy.

Who should not get shockwave therapy?

Shockwave isn't used over open wounds, active infections, or malignancy, in patients on certain blood thinners or with clotting disorders, or during pregnancy. Dr. Cedeno reviews your history and medications before recommending it.
