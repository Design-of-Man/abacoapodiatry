Peroneal Tendonitis

Pain along the outside of your ankle that grows with activity can come from irritated peroneal tendons, and Cleveland Clinic notes that untreated tendonitis can progress to a tear.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: peroneal tendinitis, peroneal tendinopathy, outer ankle tendonitis, fibularis tendonitis

Peroneal Tendonitis, explained

Two peroneal tendons run side by side behind the bony knob on the outside of your ankle. One attaches to the outer edge of the midfoot, and the other travels under the foot toward the arch. Together they steady your foot and ankle and help protect you from sprains. Peroneal tendonitis means these tendons have become irritated and inflamed, usually from repeated stress or after an ankle injury.

The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons notes that the problem shows up most in people whose sports involve repeated ankle motion, and in people with higher arches. Cleveland Clinic adds that being over 40, tight tendons, past tendon injuries, extra body weight, smoking, and conditions such as diabetes, gout, or rheumatoid arthritis raise the risk. Running, tennis, and weekend pickleball around Jupiter all put that kind of repeated stress on the outer ankle.

Common symptoms

  • Pain along the outside of the ankle or the outer edge of the foot
  • Pain that builds during activity and eases with rest
  • Swelling, warmth, or redness behind the outer ankle bone
  • A thickened or tender spot along the tendon
  • A feeling that the ankle is weak or wants to give way
  • Snapping or popping behind the outer ankle bone, which can point to the tendons slipping out of place

Causes and risk factors

  • Repetitive ankle motion in running, court sports, or hiking
  • A recent or past ankle sprain
  • High arches, which place more load on the outer foot
  • Ramping up training too quickly
  • Ongoing ankle looseness after earlier sprains
  • Anatomy you are born with, such as a shallow groove behind the ankle bone
  • Tight tendons, extra body weight, or inflammatory conditions such as gout or rheumatoid arthritis

How we treat it

Most peroneal tendon problems are treated without surgery first. NCBI StatPearls describes a conservative course of anti-inflammatory medication, ice, rest, rest or immobilization, shoe changes, and physical therapy, and suggests giving nonoperative care four to six months to let the inflammation resolve. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons lists immobilization, medication, physical therapy, and bracing as the main nonsurgical options. Surgery is reserved for tendons that stay painful after that care, or for tears and tendons that slip out of place.

Rest and immobilization

A walking boot or cast takes stress off the tendons so the inflammation can settle, which both ACFAS and Cleveland Clinic list as a first step.

Ankle bracing

A brace supports the outer ankle during sports or long days on your feet and can limit the motion that keeps irritating the tendon.

Anti-inflammatory medication and injections

Oral anti-inflammatories can calm pain and swelling. Cleveland Clinic notes that a steroid injection around the tendon is sometimes used; your podiatrist will weigh this carefully because NCBI StatPearls notes that steroids are associated with tendon disease.

Physical therapy

Guided stretching and progressive strengthening rebuild the muscles that control the outer ankle, and balance work helps if your ankle has been giving way.

PRP injection

NCBI StatPearls notes that ultrasound-guided platelet-rich plasma injections have shown improved function in tendinopathy. Abacoa Podiatry offers PRP, and your podiatrist can tell you whether you are a reasonable candidate.

Surgery when needed

If pain persists or imaging shows a tear or unstable tendon, a foot and ankle surgeon can clean up damaged tissue, repair a tear, or deepen the groove the tendons sit in.

Your first visit

At your first visit, the podiatrist presses along the tendons behind and below the outer ankle bone to find tenderness, swelling, or thickening. You will be asked to turn your foot outward against resistance to check strength, and the doctor watches for any snapping of the tendons. Your arch shape, sprain history, and shoes are reviewed, and an X-ray or MRI may be ordered to look for a tear or a related injury.

Recovery

Healing time depends on how irritated the tendon is and whether there is a tear. Cleveland Clinic says tendonitis treated without surgery typically improves in about three to four weeks, and longer if it followed another injury. After surgery, Cleveland Clinic describes four to six weeks in a cast followed by physical therapy to restore strength. NCBI StatPearls reports that athletes treated promptly with surgery may return to sport by about three months. Your own timeline can be shorter or longer, so returning to activity gradually matters.

What you can do at home

  • Cut back on running, jumping, and side-to-side sports until you are evaluated
  • Ice the outer ankle for short sessions, with a cloth between the ice and your skin
  • Choose supportive, stable shoes and avoid worn-out soles that tip your foot outward
  • Elevate the foot after long days of standing
  • Avoid uneven ground and sand runs at the beach while the ankle is sore

When to get care right away

  • You cannot walk or put weight on the foot
  • You cannot turn or rotate your ankle
  • You feel a sudden snap or pop behind the outer ankle bone
  • Pain comes on suddenly and is severe
  • The ankle is badly swollen or discolored
  • The ankle keeps giving way or feels unstable

Peroneal Tendonitis: common questions

What does peroneal tendonitis feel like?

Peroneal tendonitis usually causes an aching or sharp pain along the outside of the ankle, just behind and below the bony knob, sometimes running toward the outer edge of the foot. The pain often grows during activity and eases with rest. You may also notice swelling or warmth there. A snapping sensation suggests the tendons may be slipping, which a podiatrist should check.

Can I keep running with peroneal tendonitis?

Running through pain on an irritated peroneal tendon can make it worse, and Cleveland Clinic warns that untreated cases can progress to tears or tendons slipping out of place. Most people do better cutting back or switching to low-impact exercise while the tendon settles. A podiatrist can tell you when and how to build your mileage back up safely.

Is peroneal tendonitis the same as an ankle sprain?

No. A sprain stretches or tears the ligaments that hold the ankle bones together, while peroneal tendonitis is irritation of the tendons that steady the outside of the ankle. The two are linked, since a sprain can injure these tendons. Cleveland Clinic cites one small study of 40 people in which about 60 percent were first misdiagnosed, so a careful exam matters.

Does a brace help peroneal tendonitis?

An ankle brace is one of the standard nonsurgical treatments listed by the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons. It supports the outer ankle and limits the motion that strains the tendons, either for a short period while symptoms calm down or during sports and repetitive activities. Your podiatrist can suggest the type and how long to wear it.

When does peroneal tendonitis need surgery?

Surgery is considered when the tendon stays painful after a full course of conservative care, or when there is a tear or the tendons keep slipping out of their groove. NCBI StatPearls notes that conservative care may continue for four to six months before surgery is weighed. Procedures range from removing inflamed tissue to repairing a torn tendon.
