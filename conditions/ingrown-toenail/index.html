Ingrown Toenail

If the edge of your toenail is digging into the skin and the toe is red, swollen or sore, a podiatrist can examine it and remove the problem edge safely.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: onychocryptosis, ingrowing nail, unguis incarnatus

Ingrown Toenail, explained

An ingrown toenail happens when the corner or side of a nail curves down and grows into the soft skin along the nail border. The big toe is the most common spot, although any toe can be affected. The skin reacts much as it would to a splinter. It turns red, swollen and tender, and if the skin breaks, bacteria can get in and cause an infection.

Anyone can get one. Cleveland Clinic notes that teenagers, athletes and people with diabetes, nerve damage or poor circulation are at higher risk, and that about two in every ten people who see a provider for a foot problem come in for this condition. In a beach town like Jupiter, stubbed toes and snug athletic shoes are everyday sources of the pressure and trauma that can start the problem.

Common symptoms

  • Pain and tenderness along one side of the nail
  • Redness and swelling of the skin next to the nail
  • Pain that gets worse in closed shoes or with pressure
  • Warmth in the toe
  • Pus or fluid draining from the nail edge
  • A foul odor from the area
  • Extra, raised skin growing over the nail edge (proud flesh)

Causes and risk factors

  • Cutting toenails too short or rounding the corners
  • Shoes or socks that are tight, short or crowd the toes
  • Stubbing the toe, dropping something on it or repeated pressure from running and sports
  • An inherited tendency toward curved nails, or a nail that is wide for the toe
  • Tearing or picking at the nail corner
  • A fungal nail infection or an earlier nail injury

How we treat it

According to the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons, a podiatrist treats an ingrown toenail by dealing with both the painful nail edge and any infection. Cleveland Clinic notes that providers can often treat mild cases with the same techniques used at home, such as gently lifting the nail edge, using sterile instruments. When the nail keeps digging in, the standard next step is a minor procedure under local numbing to remove the ingrown border. For nails that keep coming back, the root of that edge can be treated so it stops regrowing.

In-office care for mild cases

For mild cases, Cleveland Clinic describes providers using the same approach as home care, such as gently lifting the nail edge, with sterile, professional instruments.

Treating infection

If the toe is infected, a podiatrist may prescribe a topical or oral antibiotic, according to the American Podiatric Medical Association.

Partial nail avulsion

The toe is numbed with an injection and the ingrown strip of nail is removed. MedlinePlus notes it takes 2 to 4 months for the nail to regrow.

Permanent edge removal (matrixectomy)

For ingrown nails that keep returning, APMA and ACFAS describe treating the nail root along the problem edge, with a chemical or another method, so that section does not grow back.

Prevention coaching

The doctor shows you how to trim straight across and what to look for in shoes, since MedlinePlus lists poorly fitting shoes and improper trimming as the most common causes.

Your first visit

At your first visit, the podiatrist looks at the nail border and surrounding skin for swelling, drainage and overgrown tissue, and checks whether infection is present. You will be asked how long it has hurt, whether it has happened before, how you trim your nails and what shoes you wear for work and sport. The doctor also reviews any diabetes, circulation or nerve problems, since those change how the toe should be treated, then explains your options.

Recovery

Recovery depends on how inflamed the toe is and which treatment you need. After a minor nail procedure, the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons says most people have very little pain and can resume normal activity the next day. The nail itself takes longer. MedlinePlus states that it takes 2 to 4 months for the nail to regrow after a partial removal. An infected toe can take longer to settle, and people with diabetes or poor circulation are usually followed more closely. Your podiatrist will give you aftercare instructions for soaking and dressing the toe.

What you can do at home

  • Soak the foot in warm water, plain or with Epsom salt, then dry it well. Cleveland Clinic suggests doing this twice a day.
  • Wear roomy shoes or open sandals so nothing presses on the toe.
  • Use an over-the-counter pain reliever such as acetaminophen or ibuprofen if it is safe for you.
  • Do not dig out or cut the nail corner yourself. ACFAS warns against this kind of bathroom surgery.
  • If you have diabetes, nerve damage or poor circulation, skip home treatment and call a podiatrist.

When to get care right away

  • Pus, drainage or redness that seems to be spreading from the toe
  • A fever, or feeling hot and shivery, along with a painful toe
  • Severe pain, redness or swelling
  • Any ingrown toenail if you have diabetes, nerve damage or poor circulation
  • Home care has not helped within a few days

Ingrown Toenail: common questions

Can an ingrown toenail heal on its own?

A very mild ingrown toenail sometimes settles with warm soaks, roomy shoes and time for the nail to grow past the skin. MedlinePlus advises seeing a provider or podiatrist if home care does not work or the nail gets worse. If there is pus, spreading redness or you have diabetes, do not wait it out. Those signs call for a medical exam.

Does ingrown toenail removal hurt?

Before any part of the nail is removed, the toe is numbed with an injection of local anesthetic, as Cleveland Clinic describes, which is meant to keep the procedure itself comfortable once the toe is numb. Afterward, the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons reports that most people experience very little pain and can resume normal activity the next day.

How do I know if my ingrown toenail is infected?

Signs of infection include pus or fluid draining from the nail edge, increasing redness or darkening of the skin, swelling, warmth and a foul odor, according to Cleveland Clinic and ACFAS. A fever or feeling shivery is another warning sign, per the NHS. An infected toe usually needs a podiatrist to remove the offending nail edge and may need a prescription antibiotic.

Why does my ingrown toenail keep coming back?

APMA and ACFAS list heredity, improper trimming and shoe pressure among the causes of ingrown toenails, and ACFAS notes that some nails become ingrown again after treatment. When the same edge keeps growing in, APMA notes that a podiatrist can perform a procedure to permanently prevent ingrown nails, using a chemical or another method on the nail root along that edge.

How should I cut my toenails to prevent ingrown nails?

Trim straight across rather than rounding or tapering the corners, and do not cut the nails too short. MedlinePlus suggests briefly soaking the foot in warm water first to soften the nail and using a clean, sharp trimmer. APMA adds that nails should not extend past the tip of the toe, and that you should never rip or tear the nail edges.
