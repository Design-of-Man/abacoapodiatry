Hammertoes

A toe that bends up at the middle joint and rubs inside your shoes is often a hammertoe, and checking it early keeps more nonsurgical options open.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: hammer toe, hammertoe deformity, contracted toe

Hammertoes, explained

A hammertoe is a toe that bends at its middle joint, so the middle of the toe rises and the tip points down. The American Podiatric Medical Association describes it as a contracture of the proximal interphalangeal joint, and it usually affects the second through fifth toes. Early on, the toe is flexible and can still be straightened by hand. Over time the tendons tighten and the joint can become rigid.

APMA notes that hammertoes are more common in women than men, and Cleveland Clinic estimates they make up as much as 20% of all foot and ankle issues. People with bunions, high arches, flat feet, naturally long toes, arthritis or diabetes are more likely to develop one. For patients in Jupiter, Tequesta and Palm Beach Gardens who move between work shoes, golf or tennis shoes and sandals, footwear is worth a close look.

Common symptoms

  • A toe that curls or bends upward at the middle joint
  • Pain on top of the toe when wearing shoes
  • Corns or calluses on top of the toe, between toes or on the ball of the foot
  • Redness, swelling or a burning feeling over the joint
  • Stiffness or trouble straightening the toe
  • Pain in the ball of the foot
  • Open sores over the joint in advanced cases

Causes and risk factors

  • An imbalance between the muscles and tendons that bend and straighten the toe
  • Tight, narrow, pointed or high-heeled shoes that crowd the toes
  • A toe that is longer than the ones beside it
  • A previous stubbed, jammed or broken toe
  • Bunions, flat feet or high arches
  • Arthritis, diabetes or an inherited foot shape

How we treat it

The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons describes hammertoes as progressive. In the early, flexible stage they respond to conservative care, and left untreated they can become rigid and stop responding to nonsurgical treatment. A podiatrist's first goal is to take pressure off the toe and calm painful corns and inflammation. When the toe becomes rigid and painful, or an open sore develops, ACFAS says surgery is the appropriate next step.

Shoe changes

ACFAS recommends a deep, roomy toe box and heels no higher than two inches, and AAOS suggests shoes about one-half inch longer than your longest toe.

Padding, splinting and taping

Protective pads over corns and calluses reduce friction, and splinting, strapping or medical tape can help hold a flexible toe in a better position, according to ACFAS and Cleveland Clinic.

Custom orthotics

ACFAS and APMA list custom orthotic devices as a way to help control the muscle and tendon imbalance behind the deformity.

Medication and injections

Oral anti-inflammatory medicine can ease pain, and ACFAS notes that corticosteroid injections may be used for an inflamed, painful joint.

Toe stretches and exercises

AAOS recommends gently stretching the toe by hand, toe-grasping exercises and towel curls to keep a flexible toe moving.

Surgery

For a flexible toe, AAOS describes tendon lengthening or tendon transfer. For a rigid toe, the joint can be fused (arthrodesis) and held with a pin or plate while it heals.

Your first visit

At the first visit, the podiatrist examines each affected toe and moves the joint to see whether it is still flexible or has become rigid, since that guides every treatment decision. The doctor checks for corns, calluses and skin breakdown over the joint and tip, looks for related problems such as a bunion, and reviews the shoes you wear most. If needed, the doctor may order X-rays to see how far the deformity has progressed.

Recovery

With conservative care, how much relief you get depends on how flexible the toe still is and how consistently you use roomier shoes and padding. These measures ease pressure and pain, and they do not straighten a toe that has already become rigid. After surgery, the American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says to expect some stiffness, swelling and redness in the toe for 4 to 6 weeks. Healing varies with the procedure performed and your overall health, and your surgeon will give you a specific plan for footwear and activity.

What you can do at home

  • Switch to shoes with a deep, wide toe box and a low heel.
  • Use non-medicated cushions or pads over corns and calluses.
  • Stretch the toe gently by hand and practice towel curls if the toe still moves.
  • Shop for shoes at the end of the day, when feet are slightly swollen, as Cleveland Clinic suggests.

When to get care right away

  • Lasting foot pain that affects your ability to walk
  • An open sore or skin breakdown over the toe
  • A toe that has become stiff and will not straighten
  • Toe pain that persists even after changing shoes

Hammertoes: common questions

Can you fix a hammertoe without surgery?

A flexible hammertoe can often be managed without surgery. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons lists roomy shoes, protective padding, custom orthotics, splinting or strapping, anti-inflammatory medicine and corticosteroid injections as nonsurgical options that ease pain and pressure. Once the toe becomes rigid, ACFAS notes that it will usually not respond to nonsurgical treatment, and surgery may be considered.

Do toe straighteners or splints work for hammertoes?

Splints, straps and medical tape can hold a flexible hammertoe in a better position and reduce rubbing, according to ACFAS and Cleveland Clinic. They help most while the toe can still be straightened by hand. An over-the-counter device will not reverse a joint that has already stiffened, so it is worth having a podiatrist check the toe before relying on one.

What is the difference between a hammertoe and a mallet toe?

Both are bent toes, but the bend sits in a different joint. Mayo Clinic explains that a hammertoe bends at the middle joint of the toe, while a mallet toe bends at the joint nearest the toenail. Both are linked to tight footwear, injury and muscle imbalance, and a podiatrist can tell which one you have during an exam.

How long does hammertoe surgery take to heal?

Recovery depends on the procedure and your health. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons says that after hammer toe surgery you can expect some stiffness, swelling and redness in the toe for 4 to 6 weeks. Your surgeon will give you specific instructions about footwear, walking and when to return to normal activity, based on whether tendons were adjusted or the joint was fused.

Will a hammertoe get worse if I ignore it?

Often, yes. APMA notes that if left untreated, hammertoes tend to become rigid, and the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons says rigid hammertoes no longer respond to nonsurgical treatment. Corns and, in severe cases, open sores can also develop where the bent joint rubs inside your shoe. Having the toe checked while it is still flexible keeps more options available.
