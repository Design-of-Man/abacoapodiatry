Metatarsalgia (Ball of Foot Pain)

If the ball of your foot burns or feels like you are stepping on a pebble, our podiatrists can find the source of the pressure and ease it.

Book Appointment (561) 915-1934

Also called: ball of foot pain, forefoot pain, metatarsal pain, stone bruise

Metatarsalgia (Ball of Foot Pain), explained

Metatarsalgia is pain and irritation in the ball of the foot, the padded area on the bottom of the foot just behind the toes. It centers under one or more of the metatarsal heads, the rounded ends of the five long foot bones where your weight rolls forward as you push off. It is a description of where the pain is, and finding out what is overloading that spot is the real work.

Mayo Clinic notes that it is common in people who run or play high-impact sports, people who wear high heels or tight shoes, and those carrying extra body weight. Bunions, hammertoes, high arches and a second toe longer than the big toe all shift more pressure to the forefoot. Cleveland Clinic adds that conditions like rheumatoid arthritis, gout and diabetes can play a part, which is one reason ball of foot pain deserves a proper look.

Common symptoms

  • Sharp, aching or burning pain in the ball of the foot
  • A feeling like there is a pebble or marble in your shoe
  • Pain that gets worse when you stand, walk or run, especially barefoot on hard floors
  • Numbness, tingling or shooting pain into the toes
  • Pain that eases when you rest
  • Swelling at the base of a toe, often the second

Causes and risk factors

  • Running and other high-impact activities
  • High heels, narrow shoes or shoes with thin soles
  • Bunions, hammertoes or high arches
  • A second toe that is longer than the big toe, or tight calf muscles
  • Excess body weight
  • Inflammatory arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis or gout
  • Related problems such as a small stress fracture or Morton's neuroma

How we treat it

Most ball of foot pain improves with conservative care that takes pressure off the metatarsal heads. Mayo Clinic describes first-line treatment as rest, ice, better fitting shoes, arch supports and metatarsal pads, and notes that surgery is rarely recommended. The American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons adds taping, calf stretching and custom orthotics for second toe capsulitis, a common source of this pain. When a bunion or hammertoe is driving the problem, your podiatrist may discuss correcting that deformity.

Pressure relief padding

A metatarsal pad in the shoe helps keep stress off the painful area, as Mayo Clinic describes, and ACFAS notes it distributes weight away from an irritated toe joint.

Shoe changes

Shoes with a wide toe box, a lower heel and a cushioned or rocker sole reduce forefoot load. Cleveland Clinic lists athletic and rocker soled shoes as useful options.

Arch supports or custom orthotics

Supports that redistribute pressure across the foot are recommended by Mayo Clinic and the ACFAS for ball of foot pain and second toe capsulitis.

Taping, stretching and activity changes

The ACFAS describes taping or splinting an irritated toe joint and stretching tight calves, while temporary low-impact exercise like swimming or cycling lets the area settle.

Treating calluses and the underlying cause

Thick calluses under the ball of the foot add pressure and can be trimmed, and Cleveland Clinic notes injections may be used for underlying causes when simpler measures fail.

Surgery when a deformity is driving the pain

If a bunion, hammertoe or a drifting second toe keeps overloading the forefoot despite conservative care, our doctors can discuss surgical correction.

Your first visit

At your first visit the podiatrist examines your foot both standing and sitting, presses along each metatarsal head and toe joint, and checks for calluses, bunions, hammertoes, a long second toe and tight calves. They ask about your shoes, activity and conditions such as diabetes or arthritis, and check sensation in the toes. If a stress fracture or nerve problem is possible, the doctor may order imaging.

Recovery

There is no single timeline for metatarsalgia, because it depends on what is causing the pressure. Pain from a shoe or training change often settles once the load is reduced, while pain tied to a bunion, hammertoe or arthritis may need longer term changes. Cleveland Clinic advises seeing a doctor if pain has not improved after two weeks of home care. If surgery is needed to correct a deformity, recovery follows that procedure and your doctor will explain what to expect.

What you can do at home

  • Rest the foot and limit long walks, runs and barefoot walking on hard floors
  • Apply ice wrapped in a towel for about 20 minutes at a time a few times a day
  • Wear cushioned shoes with a roomy toe box and a low heel, and save high heels for short periods
  • Try an over-the-counter metatarsal pad, which Mayo Clinic suggests to help keep stress off the painful area

When to get care right away

  • Pain that has not improved after about two weeks of rest and shoe changes
  • Pain that keeps you from your normal activities or keeps coming back
  • Numbness, tingling or loss of feeling in the toes
  • Foot pain of any kind if you have diabetes
  • Swelling with a toe that is starting to drift or cross over its neighbor

Metatarsalgia (Ball of Foot Pain): common questions

Why does the ball of my foot feel like I'm walking on a pebble?

That pebble feeling is a classic sign of irritation under the metatarsal heads, the bones at the base of the toes. It can come from metatarsalgia, inflammation of a toe joint called capsulitis, a thickened nerve known as Morton's neuroma, or a thick callus. An exam helps sort out which one it is, because the treatment differs for each.

Does metatarsalgia go away on its own?

Ball of foot pain often eases once the pressure on the area is reduced, for example by changing shoes, adding a metatarsal pad and cutting back on high-impact activity. If the cause is a foot shape problem such as a bunion or hammertoe, the pain tends to return unless that is addressed. Cleveland Clinic suggests seeing a doctor if home care has not helped after two weeks.

What shoes are best for ball of foot pain?

Look for shoes with a wide, deep toe box, a low heel, good cushioning and a fairly stiff or rocker shaped sole that reduces bending at the ball of the foot. Cleveland Clinic lists athletic and rocker soled shoes as helpful. Avoid high heels, narrow pointed shoes and thin soled flats, which concentrate pressure right where it hurts.

Where do you put a metatarsal pad?

A metatarsal pad usually sits just behind the sore spot in the ball of the foot rather than directly under it. Mayo Clinic describes placing one in the shoe to help keep stress off the painful area, and the American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons notes a metatarsal pad distributes weight away from the irritated joint. If a pad feels like a lump or makes the pain worse, a podiatrist can check its position.

Can metatarsalgia cause pain in other places?

Yes. Mayo Clinic notes that people with untreated ball of foot pain often change how they walk to protect the sore area. That limp can shift stress to other parts of the same foot, the other foot, and even the hips or lower back. Treating the forefoot problem early helps avoid these knock on aches.

Is ball of foot pain serious if I have diabetes?

It deserves prompt attention. Cleveland Clinic lists diabetes with foot pain as a reason to see a doctor, since reduced feeling can hide a developing callus, sore or stress fracture under the ball of the foot. High pressure areas in people with diabetes can break down into wounds, so a podiatrist should check the foot and help offload that pressure.
